Sliding-Window Streaming VLMs: Full Evaluation on Ego-MC-Bench and Wearable AI

Oct 8, 2026 completed videostreaminginteractionrecurrent memorylatency

Qwen3.8-27B and Muse Glimmer 30B run as causal online assistants (an 8-frame sliding window plus a memory they write themselves, replayed in media time) on every eligible item of Ego-MC-Bench (39 videos) and the Wearable AI validation split (2,056 sessions): about 140k model calls per model, scored with the official or adapted evaluators, plus an isolated single-stream latency study.

Overview

  • Question. How well does an offline VLM work as an online assistant when it only sees the 8 most recent frames and a short self-written memory, and what does each call cost in latency?
  • Scope. 2 models × all eligible items of 2 benchmarks: 39 Ego-MC videos and 2,056 Wearable AI sessions (EgoLongQA 700, EgoConv 656, EgoProactive 700). 140,724 scheduled calls per model.
  • Outcome. Every item ran to the end for both models; over 99.9 % of outputs on Wearable AI were valid JSON (98.6 % for Muse on Ego-MC).
  • Takeaway. Neither model handles timing well. On Ego-MC, Qwen almost never flags a mistake and Muse flags many but with low precision. On EgoProactive, Qwen interrupts too often and Muse is more balanced. Qwen answers long-video questions from its memory far better (64 % vs 31 %), largely because Muse often declines to answer. Muse is 1.9× slower per call because it reasons before answering.

Setup

Models and data

  • Models. Qwen3.8-27B (non-thinking mode, greedy decoding) and Muse Glimmer 30B (reasoning_strength = low, the model's official sampling: temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 64, seeded per call; only the to=user channel is parsed). BF16, pinned revisions.
  • Ego-MC-Bench. 40 annotated cooking videos; 39 eligible (1 has no leading instruction). Adapted per-recipe-step protocol: each instruction is released at its time and resets window and memory.
  • Wearable AI (validation split). 2,100 sessions; 2,056 eligible after structural checks (22 with a non-causal timeline, 21 whose dialogue turns do not match the questions, 1 with bad intervals). One EgoConv clip is shorter than its last annotated turn; it is kept.

Streaming wrapper

  • Window. The video is replayed in media time at one frame per second. Each call sees only the 8 most recent frames (long edge ≤ 672 px): never a future frame, never an unobserved interval.
  • Memory. A small JSON object (facts, completed steps, state, commitments, uncertainties) that the model rewrites on every call, bounded to 512 tokens. It is the only state that survives beyond the window.
  • Text input. The standing task or question, user turns released so far, and the model's own earlier replies.
  • Cadence. Memory refresh every 2 s; 10 s for media longer than 10 minutes (declared deviation). Benchmark decision times are unchanged.

Serving and compute

  • Backend. vLLM (the official Muse Glimmer image for both models), one server per GPU, 92 % of GPU memory for weights and KV cache. Calls inside one item stay sequential (each depends on the previous memory); throughput comes from running 40–64 items at once per GPU.
  • Hardware. UW Hyak Klone, mostly preemptible H200 (141 GB) plus A100 80 GB. Items are pulled from a shared queue, so preempted jobs lose no work. Wearable AI took 4.0 h (Qwen) and 7.0 h (Muse) of wall time.
  • Differences from the earlier smoke test. All items instead of 5 per benchmark, only these two benchmarks, vLLM instead of transformers, and Muse sampled as its documentation requires instead of greedy. Prompts are unchanged (v2).

Pipeline

Eight stages, from pinned sources to scored predictions. Everything before replay is deterministic and frozen before any model runs.

One replay step at media time t: the model gets only what is public by t plus its own memory, and answers with one JSON object.

  • Causal by construction. Frames are fetched by presentation time (never after t, never from unobserved intervals); questions, steps and probes are released only at their annotated times.
  • Memory is the only long-term state. It is rewritten by the model every call and bounded deterministically; it resets per item and per model, and at every Ego-MC instruction.
  • Gold stays private. Answer keys, reference replies and their timestamps are read by the exporter and scorer only.
  • Resumable. State is checkpointed after every call; preempted GPU jobs are requeued and any worker resumes an abandoned item from its last completed call.

Prompts

System prompt v2, identical for both models
You are an online video assistant watching a live stream. You only ever see the most recent frames of the stream (each labeled with its absolute time in seconds) plus a short written memory that you yourself produced on the previous call. Nothing else about earlier footage survives, so your memory must carry everything you will need later.

Rules:
- Describe only what the frames support. Do not invent events, speech, or future actions.
- Earlier frames are gone; rely on your memory for anything before the current window.
- Keep the memory compact and factual. Hard limits: observed_facts at most 8 entries, completed_steps at most 6, pending_commitments at most 3, uncertainties at most 3; every entry at most 15 words and prefixed with its time (e.g. "12s: ..."); current_state at most 40 words. Merge or drop older entries instead of exceeding these limits. The memory is for YOUR later use, not an answer.
- Reply with ONE JSON object and nothing else, in exactly this shape:
{"decision": "answer" | "speak" | "silent", "text": "<your answer or utterance, or empty string>", "memory": {"observed_facts": [...], "completed_steps": [...], "current_state": "...", "pending_commitments": [...], "uncertainties": [...]}}
- decision must be "answer" when a question is asked of you now, "speak" when you choose to say something proactively, and "silent" otherwise (then text is "").

  • Why Muse is slower. Prefill and decode speed are almost the same. Muse writes about twice as many tokens per call (391 vs 201) because it reasons in its to=self channel before the JSON answer, and its prompts are about 20 % longer.
  • Under load. During the full run, with 40–64 concurrent streams per GPU, median call latency rose to 10.2 s (Qwen) and 16.9 s (Muse) on Ego-MC and to 24.6 s and 38.6 s on Wearable AI. These numbers measure throughput settings, not the model.
  • Real time. At a 2 s refresh cadence, neither model keeps up with a live stream on one H200 even in isolation; latency here is measured under media-time replay, where the stream waits for the model.

Observations and Conclusion

  • Speaking at the right time is the weak point. The same model behaves very differently depending on the task framing. On Ego-MC, Qwen flags 2 of 392 mistakes; on EgoProactive it interrupts in 80 % of the chunks where it should and in 73 % of the chunks where it should stay silent. Muse speaks more on Ego-MC (recall 0.36, precision 0.16) and is close to balanced on EgoProactive.
  • Memory carries long videos better for Qwen. EgoLongQA questions arrive after a 10-minute stream, so the answer must come from the 512-token memory. Qwen answers every question and gets 64 % right. Muse declines 41 % of the time, and even when it answers it is less accurate (52 %).
  • Free-form answers are hard to judge without a judge. EgoConv BLEU-4 is low for both models (0.09 vs 0.05) and says little about correctness; the LLM-judge metric is still blocked.
  • Reasoning costs time. Muse's short reasoning before each answer doubles output length and per-call latency, without a clear accuracy gain on these tasks.
  • Conclusion. A sliding window plus self-written memory is enough to run both models end to end on full benchmarks, but neither is a usable proactive assistant yet: timing decisions are only slightly better than chance for Muse and biased for Qwen, and per-call latency is well above the 2 s refresh cadence.

Next steps

  • Run the LLM-judge metrics (EgoConv judge, Ego-MC feedback quality) once a judge is configured.
  • Test prompt changes aimed at timing: calibrated interrupt instructions, and asking Muse to always pick an option on MCQs.
  • Move per-call text after the frames so consecutive calls share a cached prefix (7 of 8 frames repeat), and measure the latency gain.
  • Compare against an offline upper bound that sees the whole video, to separate window/memory limits from model limits.
